What am I looking at?

The setup. A source fires photons — particles of light — one at a time at a wall with two narrow slits. Photons that get through land on a screen, and each one makes a single tiny dot. The panel on the right shows the screen face-on, filling in dot by dot.

The surprise. The dots don’t pile up into two bands behind the two slits, the way bullets would. They form stripes. Stripes are the signature of a wave: each photon travels as a wave that passes through both slits at once, and the two ripples overlap on the far side. Where crest meets crest, a landing is likely — a bright stripe. Where crest meets trough, the ripples cancel and no photon ever lands — a dark gap. Yet every photon still arrives as one dot; the wave only sets the odds of where.

Theory mode. Quantum mechanics makes a wild claim: the stripes survive only as long as nothing in the universe keeps a record of which slit each photon used. The which-path switch is that claim in its purest, idealized form — flip it, and a record exists, each photon is forced to act like it took one slit, and the stripes collapse into two ordinary overlapping heaps.

Real-experiment mode. How do you actually build such a “detector”? This is one of the possible real-world versions of the double-slit experiment (first performed with light by Thomas Young in 1801, published 1804) — it uses polarization, the direction a light wave wiggles. Put a different filter on each slit: one twists its photons clockwise (pink in the animation), the other counter-clockwise (green). The twist is a label riding on each photon that says which slit it came through. No person and no instrument ever reads the label — but merely because the label exists, the stripes vanish. This is a real tabletop experiment done with lasers and standard optics parts — see Walborn et al., “Double-slit quantum eraser” (Phys. Rev. A 65, 033818; conducted 2001, published 2002), Scientific American’s “A Do-It-Yourself Quantum Eraser” by Hillmer & Kwiat (2007), and the quantum eraser experiment overview on Wikipedia (proposed 1982, first demonstrated 1992). Other real-world variants exist too, e.g. with single electrons (first done in 1974, published 1976) or even large molecules (fullerenes, 1999).

The quantum eraser. Now add one more filter just before the screen — one that forces every photon passing it into the very same polarization. The label is wiped out before landing, and the stripes come back, at a price: the filter swallows half the photons. Rotate the eraser and the stripes slide sideways; at 90° bright and dark trade places. The swallowed half would have drawn exactly those opposite stripes — the two halves add up to a stripeless blur, which is why the tagged pattern shows no stripes until the eraser sorts the photons apart.

Fine print. Landing spots are drawn from the real optics formula, I(θ) ∝ cos²(πd·sinθ/λ) · sinc²(πa·sinθ/λ). Changing λ, d, a, the mode, or any filter resets the screen so patterns don’t mix.
